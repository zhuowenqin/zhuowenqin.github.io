I am a PhD student in the Department of Political Economy at King's College London. My research focuses on state-building and cultural change in modern China. I study how social norms and state institutions shape economic inequality, with particular interests in nation building, gender norms, and housing markets.
Before King's, I received an MSc in Public Policy from Fudan University and a B.A. in German from Nanjing University. I also spent exchange semesters at Sciences Po Paris and the University of Tübingen.

Working Papers
Hearing the Nation: Low-Barrier Information and Rural Education in Socialist China
Can the state integrate rural populations to whom its institutions remain illegible? I study this question through the rollout of wired broadcasting (youxian guangbo) across rural China after 1952, a state-funded loudspeaker network that required neither literacy nor household investment and projected news, educational, and political content directly into peasant villages. Combining census microdata on approximately 388,000 rural children with the staggered rollout of broadcasting stations across roughly 1,175 counties, I exploit within-county and within-household variation to identify effects on children's schooling. Exposed cohorts completed about 0.4 additional years of education, with effects concentrated at the junior high transition, driven by parents exposed during their impressionable years, and nearly twice as large for children of illiterate household heads.
Falling Leaves Return to Their Roots: Policy-Driven Decomposition and the Role of Clan Networks of Chinese Housing Market
(with Andong Zhuge & Ping Zhang)
We propose a new method for measuring housing bubbles in policy-driven markets and apply it to China. Decomposing city-level house prices into fundamental, policy-sensitive, and residual-bubble components over 1999–2019, we show that the residual bubble concentrates in qiaoxiang — the historical emigrant-sending regions of coastal Fujian, Guangdong and Zhejiang. We argue this reflects the cultural logic of luòyèguīgēn ("falling leaves return to their roots"): overseas Chinese remit wealth back to ancestral hometowns through clan networks that sustain kinship obligations to invest at home and coordinate collective housing demand.
Paths of Inheritance: Heterogeneity in Intergenerational Transmission of Housing Inequality: Evidence from Cohort Study in China (1935–2003)
(with Yuting Li)
Housing constitutes around 70% of household assets in China, yet the heterogeneity of its intergenerational transmission across social groups and historical periods remains underexplored. Using ten waves of the Chinese General Social Survey pooled across 69 birth cohorts (1935–2003), we trace how parental homeownership, education, and institutional status (work unit) shape offspring's housing acquisition as China moved from welfare allocation to marketization. Cohorts born after 1970 face a sharp decline in baseline homeownership probability, and the channels of parental advantage shift across regime transitions.
